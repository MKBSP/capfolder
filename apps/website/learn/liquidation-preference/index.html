Liquidation preference, explained

Written by the Capfolder team · Checked October 2026 · 6 min read

In short: a liquidation preference gives preferred shareholders, usually investors, the right to get their money back before common shareholders when the company is sold or wound down. The most common version is 1x non-participating: the investor gets either their money back or their percentage of the sale, whichever is more.

Why investors ask for it

An investor who pays $2 million for 20% of your company is betting on a big outcome. If the company is instead sold for $5 million, without a preference they would get 20% of it: $1 million, half their money back, while the founders take $4 million. The liquidation preference protects the investor in that downside case. In a good outcome, it usually doesn't matter.

"Liquidation" here doesn't only mean shutting down. In the documents it usually covers any sale of the company, a merger, or a sale of most of its assets. Most of the time it is about acquisitions.

The parts of a preference

  • Multiple: how much the investor gets back first. 1x means their money back; 2x means double. 1x is the market standard.
  • Participation: non-participating means the investor chooses: take the preference, or convert to common and share the proceeds by percentage. Participating means they take the preference and their percentage of what is left. A cap on participation limits that.
  • Seniority: when there are several rounds, either all preferred share equally (pari passu), or later rounds get paid first (stacked).

An example: Acme is sold

Acme, Capfolder's sample company, has no preferred shares today. Imagine it raises a seed round: $2,000,000 for 20% of the company, as preferred stock with a 1x liquidation preference. Then Acme is sold. Here is what the seed investor receives under two kinds of preference:

Sale price1x non-participating1x participatingEveryone else, non-participating
$4,000,000$2,000,000$2,400,000$2,000,000
$10,000,000$2,000,000$3,600,000$8,000,000
$50,000,000$10,000,000$11,600,000$40,000,000

How the non-participating column works: at $4M, 20% would be $800,000, so the investor takes the $2M preference instead. At $10M, 20% is exactly $2M, so it's a tie. At $50M, 20% is $10M, more than $2M, so they convert to common and take their percentage. Above $10M the preference stops mattering.

Participating: at $10M, the investor first takes $2M, then 20% of the remaining $8M ($1.6M), $3.6M in all. At every price, the founders and employees get less. That is why participating preferred is called investor-friendly, and why it is uncommon in early rounds today.

Stacked preferences

After several rounds the preferences add up. If Acme later raised a $10M Series A with a 1x preference senior to the seed, a $12M sale would pay the Series A $10M first, then the seed $2M, and leave nothing for common holders. That stack of preferences is the amount a sale has to beat before founders and employees see any money. Track it, and keep it in mind when you compare acquisition offers.

Why it matters for employees

Employees hold options on common stock, so they are paid after every preference. In the Series A example above, a $12M sale would leave common holders with nothing, and the options of Steve Wozniak and Bill Gates would be worth nothing, even though $12M sounds like a good result for a young company. In a $50M sale, the preferred holders would most likely convert, and common holders would share in the full amount.

This is why it helps to know your preference stack before you talk to employees about what their options could be worth, and why buyers sometimes add a separate bonus pool for the team when the stack is high.

Key terms

  • Preferred stock: the share class investors buy in priced rounds. It carries the liquidation preference and other rights.
  • Original issue price: what the investor paid per preferred share. A 1x preference is this price times their shares.
  • Conversion: preferred holders can turn their shares into common, usually one for one. Non-participating holders do it when their percentage beats the preference.
  • Waterfall: the order in which sale proceeds are paid out: debts first, then preferences by seniority, then everyone by percentage.
  • Participation cap: a limit on participating preferred, for example 3x the investment, after which the holder converts instead.

Reading the preference in your documents

The preference is not in the cap table. It is in the company's charter, the certificate of incorporation, which every priced round amends. Look for the section on "liquidation, dissolution or winding up" and a definition of "deemed liquidation event". Check:

  • the multiple (1x or more);
  • participating or non-participating, and any cap;
  • how each series ranks against the others;
  • whether unpaid dividends are added to the preference;
  • what counts as a liquidation: a merger, a sale of assets, an exclusive licence.

Most early-stage charters follow the NVCA model documents, so the clauses look similar across companies. Differences from that model are worth asking about.

Common mistakes

  • Looking only at the valuation. A high valuation with a 2x participating preference can pay founders less in a modest exit than a lower, clean 1x deal.
  • Forgetting that employees hold common. Their options are only worth something above the preference stack.
  • Not modelling exits. Work out the payout at a few sale prices before signing a term sheet.
  • Assuming SAFEs have no preference. Before converting, a SAFE holder in a sale gets the greater of their money back or the as-converted amount, ahead of common.

Questions people ask

What does 1x non-participating mean?

The investor gets the greater of their original investment back or what their shares would get if converted to common. Not both.

What is participating preferred?

Preferred that gets its preference back first and then also shares in what is left by percentage. It costs common holders at every exit price.

What does pari passu mean?

All preferred classes are paid at the same time, in proportion to their preferences, instead of the latest round being paid first.

Do liquidation preferences matter in a big exit?

Usually not for non-participating preferred: at a high price the investors convert to common. They matter most in modest exits.

Do common shareholders have a liquidation preference?

No. Common stock is paid last, after debts, preferred preferences and, in most cases, SAFE holders' money back.

Where is the liquidation preference written?

In the company's certificate of incorporation, amended at each priced round. The term sheet summarises it first.

Can a liquidation preference be negotiated away later?

Sometimes, as part of a later round or a sale, but it needs the preferred holders' consent. It is easiest to keep it simple from the start.
